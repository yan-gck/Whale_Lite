Section C. Directions. In this section, you will hear two recordings. At the end of each recording, you will hear four questions.

Recording one. When we talk about the history of medicine, we usually list discoveries, the microscope, the vaccine, antibiotics. But historians of science have become more interested in a different question, not what was discovered but who was allowed to do the discovering. For most of the nineteenth century, women were excluded from universities in many countries, and therefore from the laboratories where the important work was done. Yet a surprising amount of research was carried out in private houses. Women who could not enter a university often worked with a brother or a father, or set up a small laboratory of their own. Their contributions were frequently published without their names, or credited to a male relative. This is not simply a story about unfairness. It also explains why certain questions were never asked. Research agendas reflect the experience of the people who set them. A field studied mainly by men who had never managed a household, for example, paid very little attention to the spread of infection within families. When women entered the profession in larger numbers in the twentieth century, whole areas of medicine, child health, nutrition, occupational disease, moved from the margin to the centre. Question nine. What question are historians of science now more interested in? Question ten. Where did many women carry out research in the nineteenth century? Question eleven. What happened to women's contributions in publications? Question twelve. Why does the speaker mention the spread of infection within families?

Recording two. Let me begin with a warning. When people talk about the attention span of young people, they usually claim that it has fallen to eight seconds, which is shorter than that of a goldfish. This figure is repeated everywhere and it has no reliable source. It appears to have started in a marketing report and then been copied from one article to the next. That does not mean there is no problem. There is a real and well documented change, but it is not about the length of attention. It is about its stability. A student reading a book in nineteen ninety had few competing demands on their attention. A student reading today carries a device that can interrupt them at any moment. The interruption itself takes only a few seconds, but returning to the original task takes much longer, and the cost is highest for complex tasks. So the useful question is not how long can you concentrate, but how well can you protect your concentration. In one experiment, students who left their phones in another room performed significantly better than students who kept them on the desk, even when the phones were silent and face down. The mere presence of the device consumed attention. Question thirteen. What does the speaker say about the eight second figure? Question fourteen. According to the speaker, what has actually changed? Question fifteen. What did the experiment with phones show? Question sixteen. What question does the speaker suggest is more useful?
