Listen to part of a lecture in an art history class.

Today I want to talk about a problem that faced every painter in Europe before the nineteenth century, and that is how to sell your work. In the Middle Ages, almost all painting was made for the church, or for a wealthy family, and the painter was paid like any other craftsman. The price depended on the materials, the amount of gold leaf, the size of the panel, and hardly at all on the reputation of the painter. Now, in the seventeenth century, in the Netherlands, something changed. There were very few churches commissioning art, and instead there was a large number of ordinary, fairly prosperous households who wanted pictures. So painters began to make paintings first and find buyers afterwards. This is a fundamental change, and it has consequences that are easy to miss. If you are painting for a known client, you can choose a subject that you know that client will like. If you are painting for a market, you have to guess what anonymous buyers will want. And what do you do if the guess is wrong? You are left with an unsold painting, which is money that you have already spent. Historians have found inventories from the period listing dozens of unsold paintings in a single studio. This is why Dutch painters of that era specialized so narrowly. One painter would produce only winter landscapes, another only seascapes, another only still lifes with flowers. Specializing was a way of reducing risk, because once you had a reputation for one kind of picture, buyers who wanted that kind of picture came to you. So when we look at those paintings today and admire the realism, the careful observation of light on a wall, we should also remember that this was a commercial strategy as much as an artistic one. Question six. What does the professor say about prices in the Middle Ages? Question seven. What change took place in the seventeenth century in the Netherlands? Question eight. Why does the professor mention inventories listing unsold paintings? Question nine. According to the professor, why did Dutch painters specialize so narrowly? Question ten. What is the professor's attitude towards the realism of Dutch painting? Question eleven. Why does the professor say this: "And what do you do if the guess is wrong?"
